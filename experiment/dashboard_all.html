Overview

We asked a forecasting AI the same question — which discovery will this year’s Nobel Prize be awarded for? — four times per prize, changing only what it is shown and how it is told to use it. Each person named in the options gets a profile card of bibliometric facts (impact, defining papers, patents, textbooks, collaboration), each compared with past laureates at the time of their prize.

Reading. Attached as context to consider, the cards move the forecast no more than repeated runs without them differ from each other. Told that the cards are one of the main sources, the forecaster moves about halfway: the award favourites lose about half their probability but mostly stay near the top. Told that the cards are the main evidence, it moves furthest: the favourites collapse and “Other” grows. How much the same cards matter is set by the instruction, not by the cards alone.

The question

Which discovery will the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine be awarded for?

The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine is scheduled to be announced on Monday 5 October 2026. Each option names a discovery and the people most closely associated with it.

Options: the committee’s twelve discoveries (with up to three living people each) and “Other”. The question resolves on the discovery in the official motivation, whoever shares the prize. Its text never mentions the committee, the cards or the experiment.

Why four copies of one question

Context notes attach to a Preseen question and apply to every later run, so each arm is its own copy of an identically defined question. Arms 1 and 2 ran interleaved on 1 October; arms 3 and 4 were added on 2 October with one run each (plus one more control run that day, which stayed inside the 1 October control range).

The yardstick

Runs of the same question differ by chance. The noise unit is how far one control run lies from the mean of the other four control runs (about 0.6–0.75 percentage points per option). An arm’s run is compared in that unit; an option “moved” when it leaves the range of the five control runs.

The two instruction notes, as attached (arms 3 and 4)
Cards as one main source

Assumption for this forecast: the profile notes attached to this question are one of the main sources of evidence for comparing the named options, alongside the other evidence you would normally use.

  • Give the profiles substantial weight, comparable to that of your other main evidence: the measures they report (impact, defining works, technological translation, textbook reach, collaboration) and the reference lines that compare each person with past laureates at the time of their prize. How to weigh these measures, and the people within an option, is your judgment.
  • Use other information actively as well: prizes and awards, recent news, published predictions, the history and patterns of the prize, and your knowledge of the field. The profiles do not replace this evidence, and this evidence does not replace the profiles.
  • The profiles cover only the people named in the options. For "Other", and for contributors who are not named in an option, rely on the other evidence.
  • Take the figures as given. Do not discount a profile because author attribution cannot be audited, because the record ends in 2021, or because percentiles among past laureates are not calibrated selection probabilities; translating the profiles into probabilities is part of the task.
  • In the write-up, state for each leading option which profile evidence and which other evidence drove its probability.
Cards as the main evidence

Assumption for this forecast: the profile notes attached to this question are the main evidence for comparing the named options.

  • The profiles cover only the people named in the options. Judge each named option by the profiles of its named people; judge "Other" as you otherwise would, and use the profiles mainly to divide the remaining probability among the named options.
  • Base the relative probabilities of the named options primarily on these profiles: the measures they report (impact, defining works, technological translation, textbook reach, collaboration) and the reference lines that compare each person with past laureates at the time of their prize. How to weigh these measures, and the people within an option, is your judgment.
  • Take the figures as given. Do not discount a profile because author attribution cannot be audited, because the record ends in 2021, or because percentiles among past laureates are not calibrated selection probabilities; translating the profiles into probabilities is part of the task.
  • Use other information (prizes and awards, recent news, published predictions, the history of the prize) only as a secondary adjustment, not as the main basis for the ranking.
  • In the write-up, state for each leading option which profile evidence drove its probability.

Both are attached first with treatment=assume_true; the cards follow with treatment=consider in the same seeded order as in arm 2.

Committee & candidates

A virtual Nobel committee

Each persona is “a senior member of the Nobel Committee for ⟨field⟩ whose own expertise is ⟨specialty⟩”, mirroring the specialties of the real 2026 committees (no names). Every persona ran once on each of three models from different providers (claude-opus-5-5, gpt-5.5-2026-04-23, gemini-3.1-pro-preview), without web access, and nominated up to five discoveries with 1–3 living people. 66 ballots, all valid; cost $7.12.

Cards

One card per named person (about 300–500 words, fixed template): affiliation and option, impact (median five-year citation percentile, share of works in the cohort’s top 10 % and 1 %), three defining works, technological translation (citing inventions, own patents), textbook reach and collaboration. Every headline number is set against the field’s 2000–2025 laureates measured at their prize year. Sources: OpenAlex, PatentsView and Reliance on Science, works and patents up to 2021. A definitions note comes first and ends with “These are descriptive bibliometric measures, not forecasts.”

cards/medicine/00_definitions.md, exactly as attached

Definitions used in the profile notes

Each profile note describes one person named in an option, from the OpenAlex 2026-01 snapshot (works, citations, book citations), PatentsView 2025-12-31 (US patents) and Reliance on Science (patent-to-paper citations). The window is works published, and patents granted, up to 2021.

  • Research works: articles, reviews and letters.
  • Impact percentile: a work's 5-year citation count compared with all OpenAlex works of the same publication year and field: 0 = lowest, 0.5 = typical, 1 = highest. "Top 10 %" / "top 1 %" = percentile at least 0.90 / 0.99.
  • Disruption percentile: the 5-year disruption index (CD) of a work, as a percentile of the same cohort: high when the works citing it do not also cite its references. Works without references have no value.
  • Foundation share: the share of the works citing a work within 5 years that build on the work itself rather than on its references.
  • Citing inventions: distinct inventions (US patents and pre-grant publications) whose front-page or in-text references cite the person's works; own patents: US utility patents with the person as an inventor.
  • Textbook reach: citations from books, book chapters and reference entries; "citing books" counts distinct books.
  • Nobel laureate co-authors: co-authors (works with at most 50 authors) who are Nobel laureates in physics, chemistry or physiology or medicine; people on both sides: people who are both a co-author and a co-inventor.
  • Reference lines compare a person's value with the Physiology or Medicine laureates of 2000-2025 measured at the time of their prize: only their works published before the prize year, and only patent and book citations dated before it (undated works and citations left out). The person's own values cover the whole record up to 2021.

These are descriptive bibliometric measures, not forecasts.

Why the arms differ: how each run used the cards (summaries of the write-ups)
Cards as context

How the forecaster reasons (both arms). The question resolves on the discovery named in the official motivation, so every run ranks discoveries rather than people. It weighs the strength and recency of outside recognition (Lasker, Breakthrough Prize, Clarivate Citation Laureates and similar awards), how well a discovery fits a Nobel motivation (fundamental physiology that has led to benefit for patients), and discounts options whose credit is spread over many contributors. Awards are explicitly treated as correlated assessments of one achievement, not as independent signals to be multiplied, and the runs repeat that public awards do not reveal the committee's confidential shortlist.

GLP-1 leads in every run (17–23 %). Its hormone-to-treatment pathway is described as the strongest cumulative case on the list: the 2024 Lasker Clinical Award, the 2025 Breakthrough Prize and Clarivate's 2026 selection, joining endocrine physiology to large clinical benefit (including the cardiovascular indication of semaglutide). The main discount is attribution: several groups contributed, although a different trio of laureates would still resolve to this option.

Orexin is second (11–14 %), on fresh physiological and therapeutic validation (a new basic-science award and direct therapeutic validation of the sleep–wake pathway).

“Other” is the largest single outcome (29–35 %). The runs argue that the list omits serious contenders, naming DNA sensing (cGAS–STING), optical coherence tomography and the targeted treatment of acute promyelocytic leukaemia, and that even the strongest named option still loses about four times in five.

What the cards changed. In the treat runs the subforecasts add a passage on “the supplied bibliometrics”: they are used as a secondary cross-check and deliberately not converted into likelihood ratios, because percentiles among past laureates are not calibrated selection probabilities, author disambiguation is imperfect and there are no unsuccessful candidates for comparison. The ranking logic is otherwise unchanged. The small shifts point the way the cards' laureate comparisons do (our reading, exploratory): optogenetics, whose lead person Karl Deisseroth is above 98 % of past laureates on impact, gains 1.4 points and rises three places; GLP-1, whose first-named person Svetlana Mojsov is above 27 %, loses 1.4 points, within the range of the control runs.

Cards as one main source

The run weighs profiles and awards side by side. It states that the profiles get "influence comparable to awards and other external evidence", then runs three checks: whether orexin's 2026 milestones are substantive, whether the profiles change a headline-led ranking, and whether omitted discoveries justify a large "Other". A base-rate audit frames the awards: 18 of 26 Medicine prizes since 2000 went to a team with a prior Lasker, but only 1 of 25 Lasker Basic awards matched the same year's Nobel, so a fresh Lasker is read as readiness, not as an imminent prize.

Orexin leads at 11.7 % (control 12.1 %). The case combines its defining papers (de Lecea's hypocretin paper: disruption percentile 0.94, foundation share 0.86) with the 2026 Lasker and the FDA approval of an orexin-targeting drug in August 2026.

GLP-1 halves but survives: 20.3 % → 11.0 % (main-evidence run: 3.0 %). Holst's inventions and textbook reach are called exceptional, but the team's career-wide impact comparisons are "less uniformly exceptional than those of several competing teams"; the dense award record (Lasker 2024, Breakthrough 2025, Clarivate 2026) keeps it second.

Profile-strong options gain modestly: CAR-T 6.0 → 8.6 %, Wnt/organoids 2.3 → 5.4 %, leptin 1.9 → 3.5 %, PCSK9 2.9 → 4.1 %; the unfolded protein response falls 5.8 → 3.5 %. "Other" is 34 %.

Cards as the main evidence

The run turns the cards into a scoring model. The main-evidence run scores every named person from the card figures (impact, technological translation, textbook reach and collaboration weighted 40/30/25/5), blends the strongest person with the group mean (65:35), adds the option's defining works (20 %), and averages 81 weightings with a 10 % uniform component. It says it accepts the premises "without a vintage or attribution penalty". Prizes and news no longer rank the named options; they are used to size "Other" (40 %), which now lists optical coherence tomography, integrins, cGAS, TOR and low-complexity domains as unlisted contenders.

The award favourite collapses. GLP-1 falls from 20.3 % (control mean) to 3.0 %: the write-up cites Holst's translation figures but finds the group's impact comparisons and Mojsov's and Knudsen's book reach "below the leaders". Its 2024 Lasker and the 2026 Clarivate selection are mentioned but no longer move it. Orexin, the other award-backed option, falls from 12.1 % to 4.8 %.

Profile-strong options rise. Optogenetics (7.9 %; Deisseroth with 37 % of works in the cohort top 1 %, 103 patents, 3,009 citing books) and Wnt/organoids (7.7 %; Clevers with 2,724 citing inventions) lead the named options, followed by inherited breast-cancer genes, leptin and PCSK9 (5.7–6.4 % each). The named options now lie between 2 % and 8 % instead of led by one or two favourites.

The same-day control run is unchanged: GLP-1 18 %, orexin 12 %, Other 32.9 %, as on 1 October.

Why the arms differ: how each run used the cards (summaries of the write-ups)
Cards as context

How the forecaster reasons (both arms). As in Medicine, every run ranks discoveries by how compact and well recognised the discovery is, treats public prizes as evidence of scientific stature rather than of the committee's confidential choice, and spreads probability widely: no named discovery is allowed a dominant share.

“Other” is the largest single outcome in every run (29–35 %), because the menu omits several recognised families of discoveries; the runs stress that “Other” stands for many omitted candidates, not for one stronger rival.

Optical lattice clocks lead the named discoveries in every run (14–16 %). The write-ups call them the strongest compact discovery case: the Breakthrough Prize for their invention and development, and a Wolf Prize announced on 1 October 2026 that recognises Jun Ye (clocks) and Immanuel Bloch (optical-lattice quantum simulation); the runs split that update between the two options rather than crediting it to clocks alone, and note that the redefinition of the SI second has not happened yet.

Topological insulators follow (8–11 %), then negative-index metamaterials (7–9 %). A Nobel Symposium on metamaterials is read as strengthening that field's case without identifying the winner. Quantum information and quantum simulation receive a small timing discount for adjacent recent prizes (entanglement in 2022, macroscopic quantum tunnelling in 2025), while the runs warn against treating adjacent science as already rewarded.

What the cards changed. The treat subforecasts again discuss the supplied bibliometrics as secondary evidence (about eight references per run, against 0.5 in control) without turning them into odds. The changes are small: “Other” falls by 1.6 points (within the control range), aberration-corrected electron optics gains 0.9 points and is the one clear move outside the control range (cosmic inflation, −0.2 points, sits exactly at the lower edge of its control range), and optical lattice clocks and quantum simulators gain about 0.8 points each, within their control ranges. Unlike Medicine, the shifts are not related to how the options' people compare with past laureates on their cards (exploratory correlation 0.07).

Cards as one main source

The run writes the balance down as a formula. Each discovery gets a profile-support score B (impact, defining works, textbooks, translation, collaboration) and an outside-evidence weight R (validation, maturity, honours, current recognition, matching risk). Equal-weight logarithmic pooling gives w = exp(3B)·√R; the named options share 70 % in proportion to w and "Other" keeps 30 %.

It reports what the profiles changed: outside evidence alone would give optical lattice clocks 17 % and quantum simulation 10 %; with the profiles they get 9 % and 13 %.

Quantum simulation with ultracold atoms leads at 12.8 % (control 5.4 %, main-evidence run 13.3 %): Bloch's 181 works have median impact percentile 0.99 with 49 % in the cohort top 1 %, Cirac and Zoller add impact and textbook reach, and the 2026 Wolf Prize citation credits Bloch's lattice simulation.

Optical lattice clocks fall to 9.3 % (control 14.7 %, main 4.5 %). They keep the strongest outside weight (Breakthrough, Wolf), but Katori's textbook profile is weaker than the simulation group's.

Elsewhere: magic-angle graphene rises 6.5 → 9.2 %, topological insulators stay at 9.2 % (control 10.0 %), aberration-corrected electron optics drops 4.3 → 1.9 %. "Other" is 30 %, lower than control (33.5 %).

Cards as the main evidence

The run builds a profile ensemble. Four weightings of the card figures (career pathways, person-level balance, discovery-specific scores, an impact-heavy variant; pooled 15/35/35/15) give a probability among the named options; "Other" is set first (38 %) and the named options share the rest. People are averaged or blended with the strongest contributor, so an option does not gain merely by naming more people. Outside information enters as small multipliers: the 2026 Wolf Prize raises quantum simulation by 10 % and clocks by 20 % in relative weight.

The award favourite falls. Optical lattice clocks drop from 14.7 % to 4.5 %. Ye's impact counts for them, but Katori's textbook comparisons exceed only 27 % and 18 % of past laureates.

New order. Quantum simulation with ultracold atoms leads at 13.4 % (Bloch with 49 % of 181 works in the cohort top 1 %; Cirac and Zoller's textbook reach), ahead of negative-index metamaterials (8 %), magic-angle graphene (7 %) and topological insulators (6 %). Cosmic inflation rises from 0.9 % to 3.3 % on Linde's impact.

The same-day control run is unchanged: optical lattice clocks 15 %, topological insulators 10 %, Other 36.5 %.

Sequencing-by-synthesis leads the named discoveries in every run (18–20 %). Cards as one main source

The profiles get half the weight among the named discoveries. The run states that the supplied profiles "receive half the weight in the comparison among listed discoveries", emphasises defining work, technological translation and textbook influence, keeps career impact as substantial evidence and gives collaboration less weight. It judges a discovery through its strongest contributors rather than its weakest named person.

"Other" leads at 37.5 % (control 33.6 %): the run calls the breadth of the omitted field "the central forecasting problem".

Sequencing-by-synthesis stays the leading named discovery but halves: 19.0 % → 10.0 % (main-evidence run 5.5 %). C–H functionalization (5.6 → 4.3 %) and controlled radical polymerization (6.5 → 5.5 %) fall.

Cards as the main evidence

The run scores the profiles and calls the result spread out. The write-up allocates 62 % across the named options from the card figures and says the profile evidence "supports a spread-out forecast, not a dominant named favorite". "Other" is set at 38 % as a coverage judgment, with a stress test from 28 % to 48 %.

The award favourite falls. Sequencing-by-synthesis drops from 19.1 % to 5.5 %. Its shared 2008 paper has 1,532 citing inventions, but "Mayer's smaller scholarly record moderates the group score" (Mayer's card is one of the thin profiles). Its fresh Wolf and Princess of Asturias awards, which lead the same-day control run, no longer rank it.

Translation-heavy options rise. Efficient OLEDs lead at 9.4 % (Van Slyke's 1987 paper: disruption 0.95, 2,409 citing inventions; Thompson's 4,962 citing inventions and 219 patents, above 98 % of past laureates), followed by perovskite solar cells (7.6 %; Snaith's impact), nanoparticle delivery (6.9 %; Langer's 20,350 citing inventions) and nanopores (5.9 %). Controlled radical polymerization and C–H functionalization fall.

The same-day control run is unchanged: sequencing-by-synthesis 20 %, Other 34.6 %.

Caveats

  • Few runs. Five control and three context runs per prize, one run for each instruction arm: the comparisons are descriptive, not significance tests.
  • Arms were added one after another. The two instruction arms were written after the context arm’s near-null result, and the softer note after the main-evidence result; they are follow-up questions, not a pre-planned dose series.
  • Each note changes several things at once (weight of the cards, weight of outside evidence, scope); without a placebo (the same cards with numbers shuffled across people) moving away from the award favourites cannot be separated from following these particular numbers.
  • The options are the committee’s. Three correlated language models proposed them; a prize for another discovery resolves to “Other” in every arm.
  • Card quality. OpenAlex splits and merges authors; some profiles are thin or merged (e.g. Pascal Mayer, Robert Langer); patent counts from a name search may include namesakes. The notes ask the forecaster to take the figures as given.
  • One forecasting system on two days. The results describe Preseen on 1–2 October 2026. Scores against the actual prizes follow after 5–7 October.
