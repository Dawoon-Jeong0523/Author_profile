Overview

For each prize, the same multiple-choice question (“Which discovery will the 2026 Nobel Prize in … be awarded for?”, twelve candidate discoveries plus “Other”) was created twice on Preseen. The control question has no context. The treat question carries one profile card for every person named in the options, built from OpenAlex, PatentsView and Reliance on Science, plus a definitions note. Both were run repeatedly and interleaved; the question is whether the cards move the forecast by more than repeated control runs differ from each other.

Why each arm ranks the discoveries this way

Summary of the reasoning

How the forecaster reasons (both arms). The question resolves on the discovery named in the official motivation, so every run ranks discoveries rather than people. It weighs the strength and recency of outside recognition (Lasker, Breakthrough Prize, Clarivate Citation Laureates and similar awards), how well a discovery fits a Nobel motivation (fundamental physiology that has led to benefit for patients), and discounts options whose credit is spread over many contributors. Awards are explicitly treated as correlated assessments of one achievement, not as independent signals to be multiplied, and the runs repeat that public awards do not reveal the committee's confidential shortlist.

GLP-1 leads in every run (17–23 %). Its hormone-to-treatment pathway is described as the strongest cumulative case on the list: the 2024 Lasker Clinical Award, the 2025 Breakthrough Prize and Clarivate's 2026 selection, joining endocrine physiology to large clinical benefit (including the cardiovascular indication of semaglutide). The main discount is attribution: several groups contributed, although a different trio of laureates would still resolve to this option.

Orexin is second (11–14 %), on fresh physiological and therapeutic validation (a new basic-science award and direct therapeutic validation of the sleep–wake pathway).

“Other” is the largest single outcome (29–35 %). The runs argue that the list omits serious contenders, naming DNA sensing (cGAS–STING), optical coherence tomography and the targeted treatment of acute promyelocytic leukaemia, and that even the strongest named option still loses about four times in five.

What the cards changed. In the treat runs the subforecasts add a passage on “the supplied bibliometrics”: they are used as a secondary cross-check and deliberately not converted into likelihood ratios, because percentiles among past laureates are not calibrated selection probabilities, author disambiguation is imperfect and there are no unsuccessful candidates for comparison. The ranking logic is otherwise unchanged. The small shifts point the way the cards' laureate comparisons do (our reading, exploratory): optogenetics, whose lead person Karl Deisseroth is above 98 % of past laureates on impact, gains 1.4 points and rises three places; GLP-1, whose first-named person Svetlana Mojsov is above 27 %, loses 1.4 points, within the range of the control runs.

What's non-obvious

September recognition is not a new nomination opportunity. The Nobel timetable places the nomination deadline in January, external assessment through the following months, and committee recommendations in September. Recent Lasker and Clarivate selections therefore validate science already available for consideration; they do not show that a new candidate entered the Nobel process at the last minute (Nobel selection timetable). I also treat multiple awards for the same discovery as partly correlated judgments, not independent votes whose effects should be multiplied.

Discovery-based resolution removes much of the apparent roster risk. Habener's death on 28 December 2025 changes the possible GLP-1 laureate allocation, but does not remove the discovery from contention; Clarivate's current Drucker–Holst–Mojsov grouping supplies a different compact formulation (ASBMB memorial, March 2026; Clarivate). Conversely, matching names alone is insufficient: a cellular-immunotherapy prize centered on TIL rather than CAR-T could resolve Other despite recognizing people listed in the CAR-T option (Tang citation).

Uncertainties

The largest missing evidence is the confidential candidate evaluation, not another clinical headline. A credible contemporaneous shortlist, the committee's preferred discovery framing, and its comparative assessments would materially sharpen this forecast. Those records are unavailable under the Nobel confidentiality rules (Nobel statutes). The precursor reference class also contains only one positive observation, and archived award pages do not consistently preserve exact original announcement dates. I use it to discipline timing claims, not to estimate candidate-specific odds.

Category assignment and broad motivations remain consequential. Protein-folding mechanisms are not the same discovery as computational structure prediction, and UPR is distinct from chaperone-assisted folding (Lasker folding citation; UPR citation; Chemistry Nobel). I impose neither a mandatory field-rotation rule nor an overdue-discovery rule. The diffuse distribution reflects these unresolved choices. The six decimal places preserve normalization arithmetic; they do not imply millionth-level knowledge of the committee's decision.

Even a repaired winners-only comparison would lack the non-winning control population needed to calibrate annual Nobel probabilities. Discovery-specific evidence is more useful here than whole-career totals (MacArthur biography, Fischer biography).

Uncertainties

The largest gap is the confidential nomination and assessment record. Recommendations are submitted before the announcement, and nomination documents remain closed for 50 years. Public award convergence measures recognition, not the committee’s ranking. A deserving discovery can therefore remain unselected despite accumulating major honors (Nobel Foundation process).

The Wolf announcement is corroborated, but the precise posting time of its individual official webpages was not independently established. I use the contemporaneous October 1 reporting to support pre-cutoff availability. I found no evidence connecting the unusual announcement timing to confidential Nobel deliberations; that finding does not establish that no connection exists (contemporaneous Wolf reporting).

Category choice and motivation wording remain consequential. The Medicine and Physics announcements precede Chemistry and can change the prospects for discoveries spanning those categories. A narrower drug-delivery or synthetic-chemistry motivation can also change the matching outcome (announcement schedule).

The numerical precision is arithmetic, not predictive certainty. Holding other inputs fixed, changing sequencing’s correction factor from 1.00 to 1.60 moves its forecast from 15% to 22%. That is a parameter sensitivity test, not a confidence interval; uncertainty about the hidden candidate pool is wider. The JSON retains six decimal places for reproducibility and sums to one.

  3. One question, created twice

    Title: Which discovery will the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine be awarded for?
    Description: The 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine is scheduled to be announced on Monday 5 October 2026. Each option names a discovery and the people most closely associated with it.
    Resolution: Resolves to the option whose discovery matches the discovery named in the official motivation of the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, as published at nobelprize.org. Options are matched by discovery; the people named in an option need not match the laureates exactly. If the prize is divided between two discoveries, only the discovery with the larger share counts; if the shares are equal, the discovery named first in the announcement counts. If no option matches that discovery, the question resolves to Other.

    The question is private, multiple choice, with mutually exclusive and comprehensive options. Its text never mentions the committee, the models, the profiles or the experiment (checked automatically). Because Preseen context notes attach to a question and apply to all of its later runs, the arms are two identically defined questions; the client verifies after creation that their definitions are identical.

  4. Author profiles of every named person

    98 people (Medicine 30, Physics 34, Chemistry 34; 94 distinct) were resolved to OpenAlex author ids with the repository’s name pipeline; sixteen doubtful or unresolved cases were decided by hand with candidate tables, OpenAlex topics and ORCID records (e.g. one Emmanuel Mignot profile carries a namesake’s ORCID; Ching W. Tang and Steven A. Van Slyke are split across several ids). Each person was then profiled on Slurm: every work and patent scored against its cohort (5-year citations, disruption, Foundation share, citations from books), patent-to-paper citations and collaboration networks, for works published and patents granted up to 2021.

  5. Cards with a laureate reference

    Each card (about 300–500 words, fixed template) gives the person’s affiliation and option, impact (median 5-year citation percentile, shares in the cohort top 10 % and 1 %), three defining works, technological translation (citing inventions, own patents), textbook reach and collaboration (Nobel laureate co-authors, people who are both co-authors and co-inventors). Every headline number is compared with the field’s 2000–2025 laureates measured at the time of their prize (only works and citations dated before the prize year). A definitions note, added first, ends with “These are descriptive bibliometric measures, not forecasts.”

  6. Repeated, interleaved runs

    Per field: one control run before any context (first look) → context notes added to the treat question (treatment=consider, definitions first, cards in a fixed seeded order) → three more runs per arm, interleaved in a random order, at most three active at a time, with an idempotency key on every request.   7. Analysis

    Per option: mean, standard deviation and range per arm; Δ = treat mean − control mean against the control range; the control run-to-run spread (mean |pa − pb| over pairs of control runs, averaged over options) as the noise band; change in “Other”, entropy, rank correlation; which subforecasts moved; mentions of card measures in the write-ups; time drift between the first control run and the later ones; agreement between the committee and the control forecast. After the announcements: the log score of the realised option per arm.

Definitions note (Physiology or Medicine) · cards/medicine/00_definitions.md

Definitions used in the profile notes

Each profile note describes one person named in an option, from the OpenAlex 2026-01 snapshot (works, citations, book citations), PatentsView 2025-12-31 (US patents) and Reliance on Science (patent-to-paper citations). The window is works published, and patents granted, up to 2021.

  • Research works: articles, reviews and letters.
  • Impact percentile: a work's 5-year citation count compared with all OpenAlex works of the same publication year and field: 0 = lowest, 0.5 = typical, 1 = highest. "Top 10 %" / "top 1 %" = percentile at least 0.90 / 0.99.
  • Disruption percentile: the 5-year disruption index (CD) of a work, as a percentile of the same cohort: high when the works citing it do not also cite its references. Works without references have no value.
  • Foundation share: the share of the works citing a work within 5 years that build on the work itself rather than on its references.
  • Citing inventions: distinct inventions (US patents and pre-grant publications) whose front-page or in-text references cite the person's works; own patents: US utility patents with the person as an inventor.
  • Textbook reach: citations from books, book chapters and reference entries; "citing books" counts distinct books.
  • Nobel laureate co-authors: co-authors (works with at most 50 authors) who are Nobel laureates in physics, chemistry or physiology or medicine; people on both sides: people who are both a co-author and a co-inventor.
  • Reference lines compare a person's value with the Physiology or Medicine laureates of 2000-2025 measured at the time of their prize: only their works published before the prize year, and only patent and book citations dated before it (undated works and citations left out). The person's own values cover the whole record up to 2021.

These are descriptive bibliometric measures, not forecasts.

Caveats

  • Few runs. Four control and three treat runs per field give a coarse noise band; the comparison is descriptive, not a significance test.
  • A bundle, no placebo. The cards add many numbers at once; the placebo arm (the same cards with numbers shuffled across people) was implemented but switched off, so “more text about these people” cannot be separated from “these numbers”.
  • The options are the committee’s. Three correlated language models generated them; a prize for another discovery resolves to “Other” in both arms. Three nominees had died after the models’ training data.
  • Profile quality. OpenAlex splits and merges authors; some profiles are thin (Pascal Mayer, Bistritzer, Kurahashi Neilson) or undercounted (Mignot); patent counts from the PatentsView name search and id-matched laureate co-authors may include namesakes.
  • One forecasting system. The results describe Preseen on 1 October 2026; the subforecasts report that they gave the supplied bibliometrics little quantitative weight.
  • Exploratory analyses (the correlation of shifts with the laureate comparison) were not pre-specified.

Incidents during implementation (API keys and quotas, the OpenAlex daily budget, a stale shared cache that required one rebuild job, the Wikidata rate limit, a pause of the Preseen submissions) are logged in LOG.md and described in the manuscript.
