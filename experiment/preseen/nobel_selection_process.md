# The Nobel Science Prize Selection Process — Physiology or Medicine, Physics, Chemistry

> Prepared 2026-10-01 · Purpose: background for the KLab–Preseen Nobel forecasting experiment (virtual committee
> design, candidate profiles).
> Basis: information public in 2026. Committee rosters and announcement schedules change every year; update annually.
> Sources are listed in §11. Official material (nobelprize.org) and accounts by committee insiders take precedence;
> figures that come from secondary sources are marked as such.

---

## 1. Overview

All three science prizes follow the same three-stage structure.

1. **Nomination** — only invited, qualified people may nominate. Invitations go out in September; the deadline is
   31 January of the following year.
2. **Screening** — each prize's Nobel Committee reviews the nominations, obtains assessments from external experts,
   and over the summer writes a report with its recommendation.
3. **Decision** — a body larger than the committee (the Nobel Assembly for Medicine; the Royal Swedish Academy of
   Sciences for Physics and Chemistry) decides by majority vote in early October and announces the result the same day.

Nominations stay secret for 50 years. A prize goes to at most three people, may be divided between two different
discoveries, and since 1974 is not awarded posthumously.

## 2. Governing rules

### 2.1 Alfred Nobel's will (1895)

The object of the prize is worded slightly differently for each field.

| Field | Object of the prize in the will |
|---|---|
| Physics | the most important **discovery or invention** |
| Chemistry | the most important **discovery or improvement** |
| Physiology or Medicine | the most important **discovery** |

The will speaks of work done "during the preceding year", but in practice the prizes reward older work whose
importance has been verified over time (§8.2).

### 2.2 Key provisions of the Statutes of the Nobel Foundation

- **Two works**: a prize may be divided equally between two works, each judged worthy of a prize.
- **Joint award and the three-person limit**: a work produced by two or three people is awarded to them jointly; a
  prize may never be divided among more than three people.
- **No posthumous awards**: since 1974 a prize is not awarded to a deceased person, unless death occurred after the
  announcement.
- **Reservation**: if no work reaches the required level, the prize money may be reserved until the following year
  (rare).
- **Committee size**: each Swedish prize-awarding body appoints a Nobel Committee of three to five persons per prize.
  In practice the committees also include co-opted members with voting rights, so they work at six to eight people
  (§3).
- **Individuals only**: the science prizes go to individuals; only the Peace Prize can go to organisations.

## 3. Prize-awarding bodies and committees

### 3.1 Structure

| | Physiology or Medicine | Physics | Chemistry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prize-awarder | Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet (50 professors) | Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences | Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences |
| Working body | Nobel Committee for Physiology or Medicine | Nobel Committee for Physics | Nobel Committee for Chemistry |
| Committee size, 2026 | 5 members + Secretary-General | 5 members + 3 co-opted members | 5 members + 3 co-opted members |
| Intermediate review | none (the committee recommends directly to the Assembly) | Physics Class of the Academy | Chemistry Class of the Academy |
| Final decision | Assembly, majority vote (first Monday of October) | Academy, majority vote (early October) | Academy, majority vote (early October) |

Members serve three-year terms, and the committees are assisted by specially appointed expert advisers. According
to a former chair of the physics committee, a member can serve at most three terms. Physics and chemistry committee
members are elected from among the members of the Academy. In the chemistry committee, adjunct (co-opted) members
have the same voting rights as regular members.

### 3.3 Other Nobel Prizes (for reference)

The Literature Prize is awarded by the Swedish Academy, the Peace Prize by the Norwegian Nobel Committee (Oslo), and
the Prize in Economic Sciences (the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in memory of Alfred Nobel) by the Royal Swedish Academy
of Sciences.

## 4. Nomination

### 4.1 Principles

- **By invitation only**: the committee sends confidential nomination forms to qualified people; only those invited
  may nominate.
- **No self-nominations.**
- **50-year secrecy**: the names of nominees and other information about nominations and selection are disclosed
  only after 50 years; older material can be searched in the Nobel Prize nomination archive.

### 4.2 Qualified nominators

The three prizes follow a similar pattern; the detailed lists are adjusted from year to year.

| Physiology or Medicine | Physics | Chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| Members of the Nobel Assembly | Swedish and foreign members of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences | Swedish and foreign members of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences |
| Members of the Nobel Committee for Physiology or Medicine | Members of the Nobel Committee for Physics | Members of the Nobel Committees for Chemistry and Physics |
| Nobel laureates in physiology or medicine and in chemistry | Nobel laureates in physics | Nobel laureates in chemistry and in physics |
| Tenured professors at medical faculties in the five Nordic countries | Tenured professors of physics at Nordic universities | Tenured professors of chemistry at Nordic universities |
| Professors at other medical faculties (at least six) selected each year by the Assembly | Professors at universities selected each year by the Academy | Professors at universities selected each year by the Academy |
| Other scientists invited by the Assembly | Other scientists invited by the Academy | Other scientists invited by the Academy |

### 4.3 Scale

- About **3,000 invitations** per field. A chair of the physics committee put the annual number of invitation letters
  at about 3,500; for medicine, a 2005 JCI article reported nearly 3,000.
- For physics, roughly **300 nominees** a year are reported to be assessed (secondary source).
- The deadline is **31 January**.

## 5. The annual cycle

| When | Stage | Details |
|---|---|---|
| September (previous year) | Invitations sent | Confidential forms go to the qualified nominators |
| 31 January | Nomination deadline | |
| February | Screening of nominations | The committee groups the nominations by area and selects preliminary candidates |
| March–May | External expert assessments | In physics, 20–30 expert assessments a year, ranging from overviews of an area to opinions on specific nominations |
| June–August | Report writing | The physics committee finalises its report at an August retreat; the report proposes at most three laureates and a draft citation. The chemistry report is signed by all committee members |
| September | Report submitted and reviewed | Physics: the Physics Class discusses the report at two meetings and may propose a modification or an alternative. Medicine: the committee submits its recommendation to the Assembly |
| Early October | Final vote and announcement | Majority vote; announced at a press conference the same day |
| 10 December | Award ceremony | Anniversary of Nobel's death |

The committees' work is continuous: expert reports accumulate over several years, and work on next year's prize
starts before that year's nominations arrive.

---

## 6. Decision-making structure

- **The committee proposes; a larger body decides.** The committee has no decision power, but its report is the basis
  of the decision.
- **Physics and Chemistry**: the Class discusses the committee's proposal and may suggest changes or forward a
  different proposal to the Academy. The committee chair presents the committee's work to the Class and to the
  Academy's plenary session. The final decision is a majority vote of the Academy.
- **Physiology or Medicine**: the Nobel Assembly (50 members) chooses, by majority vote, among the candidates
  recommended by the committee.
- **Announcement**: on the day of the vote, immediately afterwards, at a press conference. For Medicine, the press
  release and the scientific background are published the moment the Secretary-General begins the announcement.

## 8. Selection criteria and practice — features that matter for forecasting

### 8.1 The unit is the discovery, not the person

A prize is given for one discovery (or two), summarised in the published **prize motivation**. Forecasting questions
are therefore best framed by discovery, with options and resolution criteria defined at that level.

### 8.2 Verification lag

Decades often pass between a discovery and the prize, and the gap is reported to be lengthening. In Physiology or
Medicine, for example, Barbara McClintock received her prize in 1983, 21 years after Watson and Crick. Work already
validated by textbooks and other major prizes therefore tends to be a stronger candidate than the hottest current
research.

### 8.3 Attribution under the three-person limit

When four or more people contributed to a discovery, who is included becomes the central question, especially when
the originator differs from the people who developed or popularised the work. Forecasts must ask not only "which
discovery?" but also "which three people within it?".

### 8.4 Split prizes

A prize can be divided between two unrelated discoveries. The 2015 Medicine prize was split between therapies for
roundworm parasite infections and a therapy for malaria; the 2021 Physics prize between climate modelling and the
theory of disordered complex systems.

### 8.5 Breadth of interpretation

The Medicine prize has interpreted "physiology or medicine" broadly, rewarding work from basic life science to
clinical medicine. "Discovery" is easier to define in basic science, while "benefit to humankind" is often clearer in
clinical medicine. The Chemistry prize also frequently goes to biochemistry and structural biology.

### 8.6 Recent prizes (2021–2025)

Used to exclude already-awarded discoveries and to see which subfields were honoured recently. Committees are often
said to avoid returning quickly to a recently awarded subfield, but this is an observed tendency, not a rule.

### 8.7 Living candidates only

Because posthumous awards are not allowed, deceased candidates drop out. When a key contributor to a discovery has
died, the prize may go to the surviving contributors, or attribution may become harder and the prize be delayed.

## 9. Indirect signals used in forecasting

Since nominations are secret for 50 years, every forecast relies on indirect signals.

- **Other major prizes**: the Lasker Awards (medicine), the Wolf Prize, the Breakthrough Prize, the Clarivate
  Citation Laureates list and similar, often read as recognition one step before a Nobel.
- **Nobel Symposia and Nobel Forum lectures**: activity around the committees and its links to later prizes (being
  compiled within KLab).
- **Bibliometric markers**: the impact of defining papers, textbook reach, technological translation (patent
  citations).
- **Co-authorship with laureates**: a significant predictor in KLab's statistical model (1985–2025). In the same
  analysis, raw citation counts and interdisciplinarity predicted weakly, and candidates cited by sitting committee
  members won more often, but so did candidates cited by former and future members, so this is not a
  committee-specific signal (team email, 30 Sep 2026).

## 10. Mapping to the experiment design

| Real process | Experiment component | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Nominations from ~3,000 nominators + the committee's first screening | Candidate list from the virtual committee | The real working committee has 6–8 people (§3) |
| External expert assessments (20–30 a year in physics) | Candidate profile cards | Quantitative assessment material from bibliometric measures |
| Majority vote of the Class/Assembly and Academy | Preseen's probability forecast | An outside observer forecasting the final decision |
| Prize motivation | Resolution criteria of the options | Resolved at the level of the discovery |

Rewritten through this mapping, the experiment asks: **does an outside forecaster change its judgment when it
receives the quantitative equivalent of the experts' assessment reports?**
