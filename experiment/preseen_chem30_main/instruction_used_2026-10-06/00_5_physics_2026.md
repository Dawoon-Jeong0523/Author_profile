# Reference outcome: 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics (announced 6 October 2026)

Awarded to Francis Halzen alone "for decisive contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy neutrinos of astrophysical origin". Halzen (University of Wisconsin-Madison) realized that the ice at the South Pole could be used to detect neutrinos, and his vision and scientific leadership as principal investigator were fundamental for IceCube, a cubic kilometre of ice equipped with light sensors. The detector came online in 2010; the first high-energy neutrinos from beyond the solar system were reported in 2013. Lag to the prize: about 13 years from the discovery papers, about 16 from the completed instrument. The discovery papers are signed by a collaboration of several hundred researchers; the committee gave the prize to the one person who conceived and led the instrument and added no second or third laureate. It framed the award as a recognition of modern, large-scale experimental science. Earlier prizes had rewarded the detection of cosmic neutrinos (2002) and the discovery of neutrino oscillations (2015); the committee treated high-energy astrophysical neutrinos as a distinct discovery.
